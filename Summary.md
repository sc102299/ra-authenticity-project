# Part 1 Summary: Stated Values from About Pages

For Part 1, I looked at how companies describe themselves on their official About, Mission, Values, Purpose, or similar pages. These pages matter because they are where companies publicly explain who they are, what they care about, and how they want the public to understand their identity. I used the Wayback Machine to collect archived versions of these pages from 2016 to 2024 for the 50 companies in the assignment. The goal was to create a historical view of each company’s stated values, rather than only looking at what the company says today.

The target was 450 company-year observations: 50 companies over 9 years. My dataset keeps all 450 rows so that missing data stays visible. Out of these, 242 company-year snapshots were found and 208 were missing. After downloading and cleaning the archived pages, 226 rows had usable page text, 208 were missing snapshots, and 16 had text that was too short or unusable.

The first important takeaway is that historical company messaging is unevenly available. Some companies were much easier to track than others. For example, UnitedHealth Group had snapshots for all 9 years. Abbott Laboratories, Johnson & Johnson, and ExxonMobil each had 8 years. On the other hand, Ford Motor, Alphabet, Berkshire Hathaway, IBM, and Meta Platforms had no usable snapshots with the URLs I selected.

This matters because missing archived pages can affect how we interpret a company’s values over time. A company with little data should not automatically be treated as less transparent or less values-driven. It may simply mean that the page URL changed, the page was not archived, or the Wayback Machine did not return a usable snapshot. For later analysis, companies with sparse coverage should be interpreted more cautiously.

Among the pages where text was successfully extracted, I compared each page with the prior available year for the same company. Most usable pages looked relatively stable. I found 137 company-year pages that were similar to the prior available page, and 45 that appeared to have changed meaningfully. Another 44 pages had no prior available page to compare against. This suggests that many companies keep their public-facing identity language fairly consistent once the page is available, but there are also moments where companies update or revise how they describe themselves.

I also coded broad value themes from the cleaned page text. The most common themes were performance and growth, people and employees, innovation, and customer focus. These appeared more often than narrower social themes like diversity and inclusion. This suggests that many company About pages frame values in broad business terms: growth, leadership, employees, customers, and innovation. Social impact and diversity language were present, but they were not the dominant themes in this first-pass coding.

For a non-technical reader, the main implication is that company “values” pages should be read as strategic communication. They do not just list moral commitments; they also present the company’s preferred public identity. Many firms emphasize themes that are broad, safe, and business-oriented. This matters for the larger project because Part 1 captures what companies say they value, not necessarily what they prioritize in practice.

This part also shows why coverage and missing data matter. If a company has few archived pages, we should be careful about making strong claims about how its stated values changed over time. The cleaner comparison will come later by combining this stated-values data with Part 2 disclosure data. That comparison can help show whether companies’ public-facing values match the topics they emphasize in more formal documents.


# Part 2 Summary: Proxy Statement Disclosure Analysis

I looked at companies’ proxy statements to understand what they emphasize in formal corporate disclosures. Proxy statements are different from the About pages in Part 1. About pages are more public-facing and often describe what a company wants to say about itself. Proxy statements are more formal documents filed with the SEC, so they tend to focus on governance, shareholders, executive compensation, risk, and oversight.

I collected and analyzed DEF 14A proxy statements from 2016 to 2024. The final dataset includes 265 filings across 42 companies. All 265 downloaded filings were successfully processed. The coverage is not complete for every company, so I treat the results as a partial but useful view of disclosure language.

The strongest pattern I saw is that employee and human capital language appears frequently across sectors. This includes words related to employees, workforce, talent, training, culture, compensation, and human capital. This makes sense because proxy statements often discuss leadership, compensation, and workforce-related governance issues. For this project, it suggests that “people” are not only part of companies’ public values language, but also appear regularly in their formal disclosure language.

There were also clear differences by sector. Technology companies had the highest average diversity and inclusion keyword rate in my results. Energy companies had the highest sustainability and environment keyword rate. Financial companies had the highest governance and ethics keyword rate. These patterns are intuitive, but still useful. They suggest that companies’ “lived values” disclosures are shaped by the issues most relevant to their industries. For example, environmental language is especially visible in Energy, while diversity language is more visible in Technology.

At the company level, the firms with the highest diversity and inclusion keyword rates included Amazon, Intel, Oracle, Apple, Microsoft and so on. This does not mean these companies necessarily perform better on diversity. It only means that diversity-related language appeared more often in their proxy statements, after adjusting for document length.

One trend over time was the increase in risk and security language. In the yearly summary, the normalized risk and security rate increased from about 1.95 mentions per 1,000 words in 2016 to about 3.94 mentions per 1,000 words in 2024. This suggests that formal corporate disclosures became more risk-oriented over this period. This may reflect a broader environment where companies face more attention around cybersecurity, privacy, regulation, and operational risk.

The main takeaway is that formal disclosures show a different side of corporate values than About pages. About pages may describe broad ideals, but proxy statements show what companies repeatedly discuss in official governance documents. In this dataset, those priorities often center on employees, governance, risk, and sector-specific concerns.

These findings should be read carefully. The method is based on keyword counts, so it does not capture the full meaning or tone of each sentence. A company may mention climate, diversity, or risk because it is making a commitment, responding to pressure, or describing a concern. Also, the dataset covers 42 companies rather than the full 50, so some companies and years are missing.

Even with these limits, this analysis is useful for the larger project.

# Part 3 Summary: Organizational Authenticity

For Part 3, I built an organizational authenticity index by comparing two kinds of company language: what companies say about themselves on public-facing About or Values pages, and what they emphasize in formal proxy statement disclosures. The idea is simple: if a company says certain values matter on its website, do those same themes also show up in its official disclosure language? I treat this as a measure of textual alignment. It does not prove whether a company is truly authentic in its real-world behavior, but it gives a structured way to compare stated values with formal disclosure priorities.

The final Part 3 dataset includes 138 company-year observations. This is smaller than the original sample because the index could only be calculated when both Part 1 and Part 2 data were available for the same company and year. The authenticity index ranges from 0 to 100, where a higher number means stronger alignment between the company’s stated-values page and its proxy statement themes.

Overall, the scores were fairly high. The average authenticity index was about 82.5, and the median was about 84.4. This suggests that, in many company-years where data was available, companies’ public-facing values language and formal disclosure language were pointing in similar directions. However, the scores also varied: the lowest score was 0, while the highest was about 98.9.

There were noticeable differences by sector. Financials had the highest average score in the results, but that sector only had 3 observations, so I would not overinterpret it. Energy also had a high average score, with 42 observations and an average score around 87.9. Healthcare also scored relatively high, around 85.7 on average. Consumer Discretionary was lower, around 78.0, and Technology was the lowest sector in this dataset, around 64.4 on average.

One possible interpretation is that Energy and Healthcare companies may have more consistent language between their public identity pages and their formal disclosures. For example, Energy companies often had strong overlap around themes like sustainability, risk, governance, and growth. Some of the highest-scoring examples came from Marathon Petroleum, Valero Energy, Chevron, American Express, and Thermo Fisher Scientific.

The lower Technology average does not necessarily mean technology companies are less authentic. It may mean that the themes on their public-facing pages do not map as cleanly onto proxy statement language using my current categories. For example, a technology company may emphasize innovation and customers on its About page, while its proxy statement may focus more on governance, risk, compensation, or cybersecurity. That mismatch would lower the score even if both documents are reasonable for their purpose. The yearly results did not show a simple upward or downward trend. Average scores moved within a moderate range across years. For example, the mean score was about 81.1 in 2016, 85.1 in 2020, 78.7 in 2022, and 82.2 in 2024. Because the number of observations varies by year, I would treat the year-to-year pattern as descriptive rather than definitive.

The highest-scoring company-years tended to have broad stated-values themes that also matched disclosure priorities. For example, top examples often included themes such as people, sustainability or social impact, governance or trust, and growth or strategy. In contrast, the lowest-scoring examples usually had a narrower stated-values vector or a weaker match between the public page themes and the disclosure themes. NVIDIA 2022 had a score of 0 in this version, which likely reflects a mismatch in the vector comparison rather than a literal claim that the company had no authenticity.

For a non-technical reader, the main takeaway is that “authenticity” here should be understood as consistency between two forms of corporate communication. A high score means the company’s public values language and formal disclosure language are aligned in this model. A low score means they are less aligned, or that the model’s theme mapping did not capture the relationship well. This index is useful because it turns a vague question into something measurable: are companies saying similar things in their public values pages and their formal filings? But it should be used carefully. The index is based on text, not behavior. It cannot tell whether a company actually follows through on its values. It also depends on the theme categories and keyword methods from earlier parts.

So, Part 3 provides a first-pass way to compare stated values with disclosure priorities. The results suggest that some sectors and companies show stronger textual alignment than others, but the index should be treated as a starting point for further review rather than a final judgment about corporate authenticity.

# Part 4 Summary: Do Changes in Stated Values Pages Relate to Alignment?

For Part 4, I wanted to test a question that came out of the earlier analysis: when companies change their public-facing values pages, does that make them more aligned or less aligned with their formal disclosure language?

This matters because a change in a company’s About or Values page could mean different things. It could mean the company is updating its public identity to better match what it actually discusses in formal documents. But it could also just be rebranding, website redesign, or a shift in marketing language.

To explore this, I used the authenticity index from Part 3 and compared scores across company-years where the stated-values page changed versus company-years where it did not change. The results were mixed. Company-years marked as `not_changed` had an average authenticity index of about 83.61. Company-years marked as `changed` had a slightly lower average score of about 80.83. At first, this suggests that changed pages may be a little less aligned with formal disclosures.

However, the median tells a more cautious story. The median score for changed pages was about 84.59, while the median for not-changed pages was about 84.20. These are almost the same. So I would not say that changed pages are clearly less aligned overall. The sector breakdown was more interesting. In Energy, changed pages had a higher average authenticity score than stable pages. Consumer Discretionary showed a similar pattern, though the difference was smaller. In Technology, changed pages had lower scores than stable pages. This suggests that the meaning of “change” may depend on the sector. For some industries, changing values language may bring the public page closer to formal disclosure priorities. For others, it may reflect a gap between public messaging and official filings.

Looking at examples also supports this mixed interpretation. Some changed pages had very high scores, such as Marathon Petroleum, Valero Energy, Thermo Fisher Scientific, McDonald’s, Tesla, and Amazon in certain years. But some changed pages had much lower scores, including NVIDIA, Intel, Amazon, Microsoft, and Tesla in other years. The main takeaway is that changing a values page is not automatically good or bad for alignment. A change can mean the company is updating its public identity in a way that matches its formal priorities, but it can also create more distance between public messaging and disclosure language.

For a non-technical reader, I would interpret this as a warning: do not assume that a new or updated values page means a company has become more consistent or more authentic. The content of the change matters. A real values update should show up not only on the website, but also in the company’s formal disclosures.

This analysis is exploratory, but it gives a useful direction for future work. A stronger version would read the actual before-and-after page text and separate meaningful values changes from simple website redesigns. That would make the authenticity index more useful for understanding when companies are genuinely updating their priorities versus only changing how they present themselves.
